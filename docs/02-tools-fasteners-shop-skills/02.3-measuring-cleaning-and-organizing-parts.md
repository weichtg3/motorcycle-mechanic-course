# 02.3 — Measuring, Cleaning and Organizing Parts

**Estimated time:** ~48 minutes  
Reading: 18 min · Required video: ~10 min · Garage exercise: 20 min

:::tip Learning objective
Calipers, feeler gauges, clean assembly, labeling and avoiding lost orientation.
:::

## Read

This lesson focuses on **measuring, cleaning and organizing parts**. The goal is not memorizing isolated parts; it is learning to see a motorcycle as a chain of inputs, mechanisms and outputs. Calipers, feeler gauges, clean assembly, labeling and avoiding lost orientation. On older motorcycles, that systems view matters because a symptom at one component is often caused somewhere else.

Good motorcycle work is controlled work. Before touching a fastener, decide what the component does, what could be damaged, what must be supported, and what evidence you need to record for reassembly. Photos, labels, bagged hardware and a written sequence reduce mistakes far more effectively than relying on memory.

Use the service manual as the authority for specifications, wear limits and procedures. Online material is valuable for understanding and visualizing a job, but model-year differences matter. If the manual and a generic video disagree, stop and resolve the discrepancy before proceeding.

### Garage exercise

Create labeled trays and practice measuring a bolt, gap and hose diameter.

Document what you find. If you perform actual service, record date, mileage, parts/fluids used, measurements before/after, and any follow-up required.

### Knowledge check

1. What is the primary system-level purpose of the component(s) in this lesson?
2. Name two faults elsewhere on the motorcycle that could imitate a failure here.
3. Which measurement or observation would you make **before** buying a replacement part?
4. Which values or procedures must come from the bike-specific service information?
5. What post-service check would prove the work is safe and successful?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answers</summary>

Answers vary by topic. A strong answer identifies the component's role in the fuel/air, mechanical, electrical or chassis system; considers upstream/downstream causes; names a measurable test; cites factory specifications for critical values; and includes a functional/leak/fastener/safety verification before riding.

</details>

## Compare the three bikes

### 🏍 1995 Sportster 883 Hugger
The **1995 Harley-Davidson Sportster 883 Hugger** is our compact Harley reference: an air-cooled, carbureted Evolution 883 V-twin with a five-speed gearbox and belt final drive. Its relatively exposed layout makes it especially useful for learning mechanical relationships. Exact specifications and procedures must be confirmed against the correct 1995 factory literature for the VIN/configuration.

### 🏍 1996 Ultra Classic
The **1996 Harley-Davidson Electra Glide Ultra Classic (FLHTCU)** adds touring complexity. It uses a carbureted 1340 Evolution engine and belt final drive, but the fairing, luggage, touring electrical loads and greater mass make access, electrical diagnosis and chassis work more demanding. A current parts reference lists the 1340 carbureted Evolution engine and belt drive for this model, but factory literature remains authoritative.

### 🏍 1998 Shadow Aero 1100
The **1998 Honda Shadow Aero 1100** gives us a deliberately different engineering approach: Honda documented an 1,099 cc liquid-cooled four-stroke V-twin, five-speed transmission and shaft final drive. That makes it ideal for comparing cooling, packaging and final-drive service with the Harleys.
